

Introduction:

This project modifies the Single-Layer Perceptron (SLP) developed in class to the MNIST handwritten digit
dataset and compares it against the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). The task is a ten-class classification
problem: given the 784 pixel values of a 28 x 28 grayscale image, predict which digit, 0 through 9, was
written.

Apart from accuracy, the primary objective of the project was to investigate two design decisions and report what
they change. First, the learning rate of the SLP is varied across 0.001, 0.01 and 1.0 while every other setting
is held fixed, to observe its effect on the training process and on final performance. Second, the
hidden-layer architecture of the MLP is varied across one hidden layer of 32, 64, and 128 neurons and a
two-layer 64-32 configuration, to observe how added capacity affects both training accuracy and
generalization to unseen images. The two model families are then compared directly using the same
train/test data, with confusion analysis and an inspection of individual correct and incorrect predictions.

Summary (Part 1)
• Did changing the learning rate affect the training process?
	- Changing the learning rate did slightly affect the training process. The higher learning rates began with an initial higher accuracy, but after the first 7 epochs, 
	the 3 rates converged to an equal training accuracy around 79.2%. Past epochs 7 and 8, the lowest learning rate, 0.001, was the most accurate by a slight margin. 

• Did it affect the final training or test accuracy?
	- Yes, lower learning rates resulted in slightly higher training and test accuracies 
• Which learning rate produced the best results, if any?
	- Although by a small margin, learning rate 0.001 produced the most accurate results. However, it was only more accurate after 8 epochs
• If the results were similar or unchanged, what did you observe about the learning
process?
	- For this study, lower learning rates result in higher errors for the first few epochs, but eventually yield higher accuracies than
	higher learning rates, whose accuracy plateaus after the first few epochs.

Digit Analysis (Part 3)
• Which digits are most frequently confused?
	- 3 and 9 were both in the top 3 most confused digits for both the SLP with learning rate 0.001 and the MLP with one hidden layer with size 128
• Does the SLP struggle with particular digits?
	- Yes, 9, 8, 3, and 5 were all digits it struggled with heavily. The MLP model greatly reduced the number of false predictions from mid 200s to the 40-50 range for those digits.
• Does the MLP improve the classification of those digits?
	- Yes. Those digits are still the most difficult to classify for the MLP, but the overall classification accuracy has significantly increased with those digits.
• Is there a noticeable difference between training and test accuracy?
	- For the SLP, yes. The training accuracy was 79.63%, which resulted in a test accuracy of 89.47%, whereas for the MLP model, the training accuracy was 99.98 % with
	a test accuracy of 97.96%

Individual Predictions
	- Relies on unique parts of numbers to classify that number. However, when the numbers are written with different handwriting or style, certain features may resonate with a different number to which the feature is more common to.
	- It is possible that the model associates the pattern of thick, central clusters of pixels with the digit 8 as the cluster often forms in the middle of the 8 where the 	lines intersect. However, many 3s and 5s were confused with the number 8, as they also may have a similar central thickness, which may trick the model into 	classifying the digit as an 8.
	- 9s, 7s, and 1s were also confused in cases where someone writes the number 1 with a slant at the top, or if someone writes a 9 with a thin oval instead of circle, 	
	both have been classified as 7s on many occasions, since the slant line pattern is universally more common to a 7 then it is to a 1 or 9, which can be drawn without the 
	resembling pattern.

Final Analysis (Part 4)
• Network Architecture: How is the architecture of the MLP different from the SLP?
	- The MLP has a hidden layer of 128, which takes in the learned weights from the 784 pixels, and learns them once more to develop more accurate weights to be 
	condensed into 10 digits.

• Hidden Layers: What role do hidden layers play?
	- Hidden layers play the same role as the neurons in the SLP, except that instead of taking in the raw data, the hidden layer assesses the output from the first layer 	of neurons to fine-tune the weights even further, allowing for more complex pattern recognition from the model, which can lead to higher accuracies. In this case, the 	hidden layer size refers to the number of neurons that will each assess all 784 pixels with different weights to discover patterns that can be used to classify digits, 	such as curves, strokes, and angles.

• Performance: Which model achieved better test performance? How large was the
difference?
	- The MLP yielded better test accuracy (97.96%) than the SLP (85.89%)

• Model Complexity: How did changing the number of hidden neurons and adding a
second hidden layer affect model performance?
	- All models with a hidden layer performed better, with the worst one resulting in a test accuracy of 96.50%. Adding two hidden layers (64,32) only performed
	better then the model with a hidden layer size of 32, but not the models with sizes 64 and 128.

• Generalization: Compare training and testing performance. What does this tell you
about how well the models generalize to unseen images?
	- The MLP models tested a bit worse than their training accuracy, whereas the SLP's testing accuracy was always better than their training accuracy. 
	This shows that although the MLP is overall more accurate, it can overfit the training data at times, whereas the SLP model performs better on unseen data, showing that 
	it does not overfit the training data.

• Limitations: What limitations did you observe in the SLP? How did the MLP
address some of these limitations?
	- The SLP could not match the MLP in accuracy, as the MLP improved the accuracy of all digit predictions. The SLP was more easily confused between common features of
	digits with similar structures, whereas it took sloppy handwriting to confuse the MLP models

Conclusion:
Across the three SLP runs, the learning rate changed the shape of the training curve more than it changed
the endpoint of training. The smallest rate, 0.001, carried the most errors through the first seven epochs
yet finished with the best training accuracy (79.63%), while the largest rate, 1.0, fell fastest and then
flattened from the fourth epoch onward. By epoch 8 all three runs sat at roughly the same training
accuracy. The clearer separation appeared on unseen data, where lower learning rates produced higher test
accuracy: 89.47% at 0.001 against 85.89% at 1.0.

Adding a hidden layer mattered far more than tuning the learning rate. Every MLP architecture beat the
SLP on the test set, with the weakest of them still reaching 96.50%. The best performer on unseen data was
the 784 → 128 → 10 network at 97.96%, roughly eight and a half percentage points above the SLP.
Widening a single hidden layer helped steadily from 32 to 64 to 128 neurons, whereas splitting capacity
across two layers (64, 32) landed below both the 64 and 128 single-layer networks, so extra depth by itself
was not the deciding factor.

The two model families also failed in different ways. The SLP's test accuracy was consistently higher than
its training accuracy and it did not overfit, but it was easily confused between digits that share structural
features; 9, 8, 3,5 as well as 9, 7, 1 accounted for its heaviest error counts. The MLP reversed that pattern, training
almost perfectly and testing slightly lower, which shows it can overfit, while cutting false predictions on
those same digits from the mid 200s down to the 40-50 range. Notably, 3 and 9 remained among the top
three most confused digits for both models: the hidden layer reduced the severity of those confusions
without eliminating their cause, which the individual predictions trace back to handwriting styles or quality whose
strokes genuinely resemble another digit.
